Security Frameworks

Learning outcomes:

  • Explain what a security framework is, and why organizations use one
  • Compare common frameworks, such as the NIST Cybersecurity Framework, ISO/IEC 27001, the CIS Controls, and MITRE ATT&CK
  • Map an attack or a security task to a framework, such as an attacker's steps to MITRE ATT&CK or a control to the NIST CSF functions
  • Recommend a framework for an organization, based on its size, industry, and compliance needs

  • What Is a Security Framework?
    • A shared set of guidelines and best practices for managing security risk
    • Gives teams a common language, a checklist, and a way to measure progress
    • A framework is guidance. Compliance is what a law or contract requires. They overlap, but they aren't the same thing
  • Why Use One?
    • You don't have to invent your own process from scratch
    • Easier to explain security to leadership, auditors, and customers
    • Shows where the gaps are, and what to fix first
  • NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0
    • Free, widely used in the US, and meant for any size of organization
    • 6 functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover
    • Profiles and tiers show where you are now, and where you want to be
    • NIST's quick start guides, including one for small businesses
  • ISO/IEC 27001
    • The international standard for an information security management system
    • Organizations can be certified, which is often needed for international business
    • More process-heavy than the NIST CSF, so it's a bigger commitment
  • CIS Controls
    • A prioritized list of practical security actions
    • Implementation Groups (IG1 to IG3) based on an organization's size and resources. IG1 is a good starting point for a small business
    • Links to the CIS Benchmarks from the Hardening topic
  • MITRE ATT&CK
    • A knowledge base of real attacker tactics and techniques, based on real attacks
    • Red teams use it to plan tests. Blue teams use it to check what they can detect
    • Connects to the pen testing stages from earlier in the course: reconnaissance maps to the Reconnaissance tactic, enumeration to Discovery, and exploitation to tactics such as Initial Access and Execution
    • ATT&CK Navigator lets you highlight techniques and build your own map
  • Lockheed Martin Cyber Kill Chain
    • 7 stages of an attack, from reconnaissance to actions on objectives
    • Older and simpler than ATT&CK, but still common in reports and job interviews
    • The idea: break any link in the chain, and the attack fails
  • Other Frameworks and Regulations You'll See
    • NIST SP 800-53: a detailed catalog of security controls, required for US federal systems
    • PCI DSS: required for anyone who handles card payments
    • HIPAA: required for US healthcare organizations that handle patient data
    • SOC 2: an audit report that service providers give their customers to show their security controls work
  • Choosing a Framework
    • Industry and laws: healthcare, finance, and government each have their own requirements
    • Size and budget: CIS IG1 for a small business, against full ISO 27001 for a large one
    • Customers: many customers ask for a SOC 2 report or ISO 27001 certification before they'll sign a contract
    • Many organizations use more than one, and map them together. For example, NIST CSF for the big picture, and CIS Controls for the day to day actions
  • Frameworks and the Rest of This Course
    • Security audits often check an organization against a framework
    • Hardening uses CIS Benchmarks, which support the CIS Controls
    • Incident response and recovery match the NIST CSF Respond and Recover functions

Suggested Activities and Discussion Topics:

  • Activity: map each stage of a pen test from this course (reconnaissance, enumeration, exploitation, and so on) to a tactic in MITRE ATT&CK. Which stages map easily, and which don't?
  • Activity: a 10 person accounting office asks which framework to use. Recommend one, and explain why, using its size, industry, and budget.
  • Activity: pick 3 steps from the Incident Recovery topic, and match each one to a NIST CSF function. Which function has the most steps?
  • Discussion: is following a framework the same as being secure? Can an organization be compliant and still get breached?
  • Activity: use the ATT&CK Navigator to highlight the techniques used in a recent attack in the news. Share your map and one detection idea for it.
  • Discussion: compare the Cyber Kill Chain with MITRE ATT&CK. Which would you use to explain an attack to leadership, and which to a security team? Why?
