Social Engineering

Learning outcomes:

  • Recognize common social engineering attacks such as phishing, smishing, vishing, pretexting, and tailgating in realistic workplace scenarios
  • Analyze a suspicious email or message and identify the warning signs that it is malicious
  • Describe how attackers use publicly available information to make social engineering attacks more convincing
  • Explain how to plan and run a phishing awareness campaign, including measuring results and following up with staff
  • Describe how to report and respond to a suspected social engineering attempt within an organization

  • Why Attackers Target People
    • It's easier to trick someone than to break encryption
    • Most breaches start with a human element
    • Phishing keeps growing every year, see the APWG Phishing Activity Trends Reports
    • Everyone is a target: employees, students, family members
  • The Psychology Behind It
    • Urgency ("act now or your account is closed")
    • Authority ("this is IT" or "this is your CEO")
    • Fear, curiosity, helpfulness, trust
  • Common Attack Types
    • Phishing, spear phishing, whaling
    • Smishing (text) and vishing (phone)
    • Pretexting and impersonation
    • The Social Engineering Framework breaks down these attacks and the psychology behind them
  • Physical Social Engineering
    • Tailgating and piggybacking
    • Fake delivery people, contractors and "new employees"
    • Dropped USB drives
  • How Attackers Do Their Homework
    • LinkedIn, company websites, social media
    • Job postings reveal the software and systems in use
    • Personal posts give away answers to security questions
  • Spotting a Malicious Message
    • Check the sender's address, not just the display name
    • Hover over links before clicking
    • Unexpected attachments, odd tone, pressure to act fast
    • The FTC's guide to recognizing and avoiding phishing scams
    • Example: walk through a real phishing email
  • AI and Modern Social Engineering
    • Phishing emails with no spelling mistakes
    • Voice cloning and deepfake video calls
    • Why "it sounded just like them" is no longer proof
  • Personal Protection
    • Lock down social media privacy settings
    • Verify requests through a different channel (call back on a known number)
    • Family code words for emergency calls
    • Use MFA everywhere
    • CISA's tips for avoiding social engineering and phishing attacks
  • Running a Phishing Awareness Campaign at Work
    • Get leadership buy-in and set clear goals
    • NIST SP 800-50 covers how to build a security awareness and training program
    • Send simulated phishing emails
    • Measure click rates and report rates over time
    • Train people instead of shaming them
    • CISA's phishing guidance for organizations
  • Reporting and Responding
    • Make reporting easy (a report-phish button, a clear contact)
    • What to do if you clicked: report quickly, don't hide it
    • How security teams triage and respond to reports
    • Scams can be reported to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), which also publishes yearly reports on losses

Suggested Activities and Discussion Topics:

  • Discussion: share a scam attempt you or someone you know received. What warning signs were there?
  • Activity: take the Google Phishing Quiz. Which ones fooled you, and why?
  • Activity: look at your own social media from an attacker's point of view. What could someone use to build a convincing pretext against you?
  • Activity: review the example emails and text messages. For each one, decide if it is a scam or legitimate and list the warning signs before you open the red flags.
  • Discussion: should companies discipline employees who repeatedly fail phishing tests? What is more effective, consequences or more training?
